Research directions

My research asks what controls land-surface responses to climate extremes, how long those effects persist, and how to represent them in Earth system models. Interpretable and physics-guided machine learning connects three directions.

01

Land-surface exchange and environmental memory

I investigate how antecedent water and thermal conditions, together with vegetation structure, shape evapotranspiration and surface energy partitioning. Combining observations with interpretable models, I aim to identify the physical processes and timescales that land models need to represent.

Related work: water and heat exchange
02

Vegetation responses to extremes and recovery

Building on memory-aware models and a NASA-funded project, I will examine when antecedent conditions buffer or amplify ecosystem responses to rainfall pulses, heatwaves, and drought. I want to understand how ecological thresholds govern carbon uptake, water use, and recovery after an event.

Related work: environmental memory
03

Carbon–water–energy feedbacks in Earth system models

I will use observation-constrained, differentiable modeling to incorporate environmental memory and ecological thresholds into land models while conserving mass and energy. I will test how these representations affect predictions of carbon sinks, water use, extremes, and land–atmosphere feedbacks.

Related work: DifferLand

Selected research contributions

Three areas of published work provide the foundation for these directions.

01

Water & heat exchange
GRL · 2019
ERL · 2026

Physics-guided modeling of water and heat exchange

I integrated surface energy-balance and Penman–Monteith physics into machine learning for evapotranspiration. The model conserved surface energy and generalized better to hot and dry extremes excluded from training.

In student-led work, we also developed a critical canopy-temperature framework to improve remote-sensing estimates of evapotranspiration.

GRL paper ↗
02

Environmental memory
HESS · 2026
WRR · 2026

Environmental memory in responses to climate extremes

I developed an explainable recurrent model of evaporative fraction that uses meteorological histories to represent responses to rainfall and subsequent drying. Differences in memory among ecosystems suggested roles for water storage and plant water-use strategies.

For snow, coupling a land model with an observation-constrained recurrent model improved snow water equivalent simulations, especially in mountain environments.

HESS paper ↗
03

Differentiable modeling
Nature Communications
2026

Observation-constrained differentiable land modeling

I co-developed DifferLand, a differentiable land model that learns environmental controls on ecological functions and carbon–water exchange from global observations. My contributions included parameter-identifiability analysis and interpretation.

I am applying this framework to hydroclimatic pulses and Amazon forest responses to El Niño, examining carbon–water coupling and recovery.

Implications

Understanding land-surface responses to climate extremes can inform ecosystem resilience, water-resource assessment, and urban heat adaptation. My related work on electricity systems examines how extreme weather affects fossil-fuel reliance and emissions.

  • Temperature extremes and fossil-fuel reliance — our analysis found increased reliance on fossil fuels during extreme-temperature events in the continental United States.
  • ECON-PowerCI — a collaborative dataset tracking daily European electricity consumption and carbon intensity.
  • Building façade photovoltaics — collaborative research on photovoltaics and climate resilience.
